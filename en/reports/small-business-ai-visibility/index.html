How independent shops and small companies
get found by AI

Kyoto is full of shops and companies run by one person, or by a handful of people. People who've put their energy into the daily work rather than into big advertising. Never interviewed, never featured on a ranking list.

How do businesses like these get found by generative AI? A few things have become clear from the observations so far.

Where comparison media is thin, a business's own information carries weight

Monthly Report #000 (observed July 2026) looked at Kyoto experience businesses across three categories — tea ceremony, kimono rental, zazen meditation — in four languages: Japanese, English, Traditional Chinese, Simplified Chinese. Across 240 runs, one thing stood out: which sources AI relied on varied sharply by category.

For kimono rental, where comparison sites and travel media are already well established, AI leaned heavily on third-party articles (53.7% in Japanese, 49.1% in English, driven by comparison media). It's hard for an independent shop to stand out inside that structure.

Zazen meditation looked different. Across all four languages, comparison media never took the lead in citations. Instead, public institutions — Kyoto City's official site, for instance — or the temples' and businesses' own information dominated (80.2% self-published in English, 49.5% in Simplified Chinese).

A category with thin comparison coverage is, seen the other way, one where a business isn't buried under big media. There, how well a business organized and published its own information mapped directly onto the outcome.

Whether you get found wasn't a matter of scale

An earlier observation (Feature: "It had great Google reviews. The AI said it didn't exist.") looked at how AI recognized ten Kyoto experience businesses.

What it found: review count and business size didn't reliably predict whether AI understood a business correctly. Larger businesses were sometimes confused with a different business when their official information was buried inside a booking platform's listing page. Smaller businesses, when their own information was well organized, were understood and cited accurately.

What AI was looking at wasn't scale. It was whether information about the business itself existed in a form AI could cite.

What you can check for your own business

From these two observations, an independent shop or small company that can't — or doesn't want to — rely on comparison sites has two things worth checking first:

  • Is information like pricing, services, location, and how to book laid out clearly in one place?
  • Is that information written in the business's own voice, speaking for itself, rather than appearing as one line item inside a booking platform or directory page?

This doesn't require special technical skill or budget. Reorganizing what's already on a website or social media into a form AI can read is often enough to change the picture.

Of course, this alone doesn't guarantee being recommended. In categories where comparison media is well established, organizing your own information isn't enough to overturn that structure. But in categories where comparison media hasn't developed, or when someone asks about a business by name, it makes a difference.

Kääselä faces the same question

Kääselä is also a small research project, run by one person, with no budget. The same challenge applies here too.

No advertising spend. No effort spent polishing a social media presence. No exaggerated claims, no gray-area ranking tricks. Just organizing what's genuinely true about the project, in a form AI can understand, on its own site. Kääselä believes that's not a detour, that it eventually reaches customers just the same. The same question will keep being asked of Kääselä itself going forward, and that record will be published in a form useful to other businesses facing the same challenge.
