Appendix: Noise-Floor Method and Source Taxonomy

A permanent appendix referenced by every issue of the Kääselä Monthly Report. Individual issues link here rather than restating the method.

1. Why we measure the noise floor

Generative AI doesn't necessarily give the same answer to the same question twice. When an observation in a given month appears to show "the AI's citations changed," we can't tell whether that's a real shift or simple generative noise — not without first knowing how large that noise is.

This study measures the size of that noise ahead of time and uses it as a baseline for reading month-to-month differences. What separates this from commercial tools that declare "the AI says X" from a single observation (n=1) is that we measure this baseline ourselves and publish it.

2. Measurement method

Each query was run 20 times in a row (n=20) under identical conditions (ChatGPT, GPT-5.5, web version, temporary chat, keyword-style query, executed from Kyoto), recording the set of cited source domains on each run.

The overlap between the cited-source sets of any two runs was expressed as a Jaccard coefficient (size of the intersection ÷ size of the union). A value near 1.0 means the same sources are cited every time; a value near 0 means the sources change every time.

The highest variance is kimono rental in Traditional Chinese (0.11), because citations are spread across many independent business sites. The most stable cell is Zen meditation in English (0.40), where citations concentrate on a small number of temple sites.

The Japanese and English tea-ceremony values (0.247 / 0.204) land close to the figure reported by an independent external study (Green et al., 0.265). This is one piece of corroborating evidence that this study's noise floor, measured independently, is consistent with other observations.

The noise floor varies by category and language. A single threshold cannot be applied uniformly across all cells.

4. Pre-registered significance threshold

Using a bootstrap method, we confirmed that the citation-source category mix (our primary metric) falls within a 95% interval of roughly ±10 points at n=20. When comparing two points in time, a change of about 14 points or more is treated as a "signal."

This threshold was fixed and registered before observation began for Issue #1 (September 2026) and will not change afterward. Adjusting the threshold after seeing results would defeat the purpose of pre-registration, so we don't do that.

Individual businesses' appearance rates vary more than the category mix (roughly ±18–22 points); month to month, we only evaluate the concentration of the top group.

Classification is based on the domain's own primary function, not how it's used within a given answer. Whether a source has affiliate links doesn't determine its category; it's recorded as a separate flag.

6. Limitations of the method

Limitation one: the candidate pool is not disclosed. This study is based on manual observation through ChatGPT's standard UI. The pages the AI searches and considers (the grounding pool) and the pages ultimately cited in the answer (citations) are separate layers, and UI-based observation only captures the latter. We cannot measure each business's selection or rejection rate against the candidate pool — only its frequency of appearance across n trials. To avoid implying a selection rate we can't measure, this study consistently uses "appearance rate" rather than "selection rate."

Limitation two: what we mean by reproducibility. In this study, reproducibility means agreement in the statistical distribution of results, not verbatim agreement. Because of generative randomness (addressed via n=20), engine version changes, and differences in account, region, and personalization, individual trials under the same procedure may not match. Claims of reproducibility are limited to: "under the same time window, the same engine version, and with personalization excluded, a statistically consistent distribution is obtained."
